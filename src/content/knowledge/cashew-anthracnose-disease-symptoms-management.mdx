## 1. Introduction

Cashew (*Anacardium occidentale L.*) is an important agricultural crop in Cambodia, providing income to farmers and supporting the country's agricultural economy. However, cashew trees can be affected by several diseases that reduce plant health, damage developing shoots and flowers, and threaten production.

One of these diseases is anthracnose, a fungal disease associated with species of *Colletotrichum*. It has been documented in cashew-producing countries including Brazil, India, and several African countries.

Anthracnose can affect tender leaves, young shoots, flower clusters, cashew apples, and developing nuts. Severe infections may damage reproductive structures and reduce the quantity or quality of harvestable produce.

Early recognition, correct diagnosis, and appropriate orchard management are essential for reducing disease risks.

This article explains the symptoms, conditions that favor infection, and practical management approaches that Cambodian cashew growers can consider.

## 2. What Causes Cashew Anthracnose?

Cashew anthracnose is associated with fungi belonging to the genus *Colletotrichum*.

Older agricultural references commonly identify *Colletotrichum gloeosporioides* as the causal organism. However, more recent research has shown that several closely related *Colletotrichum* species may be associated with anthracnose symptoms in cashew.

These fungi can infect susceptible plant tissues and cause damage under favorable environmental conditions.

The disease is particularly important when young leaves, shoots, flower clusters, and developing fruits are exposed to infection.

Correct identification matters because different plant diseases and insect injuries may produce similar symptoms.

A scientific review published in *Agronomy* in 2022 emphasized that inaccurate identification of cashew pathogens can lead to inappropriate disease management.

Therefore, visible symptoms should be treated as warning signs rather than definitive proof of anthracnose.

## 3. Early Symptoms of Anthracnose

The appearance of anthracnose depends on the affected plant part and the stage of infection.

### 3.1 Symptoms on leaves

Young leaves may develop reddish-brown or dark brown spots that gradually enlarge.

Some affected areas may appear water-soaked before becoming dry and necrotic.

Severely affected young leaves may become distorted, curled, or prematurely dry.

However, leaf spots can also result from other diseases, insect damage, chemical injury, or environmental stress.

### 3.2 Symptoms on young shoots and twigs

Infected shoots may develop dark lesions that expand along tender tissues.

As the infection progresses, affected shoots may dry out or die back.

Repeated damage to growing shoots can interfere with canopy development, especially in young trees.

### 3.3 Symptoms on flowers

Flower clusters may develop dark spots or blighted areas.

Affected flowers and portions of the flower cluster may dry and fall prematurely.

Damage during flowering is particularly concerning because it may reduce successful fruit and nut development.

### 3.4 Symptoms on cashew apples and nuts

Cashew apples may develop dark, irregular, or sunken lesions.

In severe cases, affected apples may shrivel or decay.

Developing nuts may also show dark spotting, and heavily affected reproductive structures may drop before maturity.

These symptoms require careful assessment because insect feeding, other pathogens, and physical damage may produce similar appearances.

## 4. Environmental Conditions and Infection Risks

Anthracnose development is influenced by the interaction between the pathogen, the cashew tree, and environmental conditions.

### 4.1 Rainfall and prolonged moisture

Rainfall, splashing water, and prolonged wetness on plant surfaces can favor infection and the spread of fungal spores.

Periods of frequent rain may therefore increase disease risk, especially when susceptible young tissues are present.

### 4.2 Humidity and canopy conditions

Dense canopies and poor air movement may allow leaves and shoots to remain wet for longer periods.

These conditions can create an environment favorable to several fungal diseases.

However, humidity alone does not confirm the presence of anthracnose.

### 4.3 Flowering and new growth

New shoots, tender leaves, and flower clusters are important areas for monitoring because they may be vulnerable to infection.

Rainfall occurring during flowering can increase concern about disease-related damage.

### 4.4 Infected plant material

Affected shoots, leaves, and other plant tissues may contribute to the persistence of fungal inoculum.

Good orchard sanitation can help reduce potential sources of infection.

Farmers should remember that disease development varies according to local weather, plant condition, and the pathogen involved.

## 5. Distinguishing Anthracnose from Other Problems

Not every brown spot, blackened flower, or dying shoot is caused by anthracnose.

Several problems can produce overlapping symptoms.

**Tea mosquito bug damage:** Feeding injuries may cause dark lesions on tender shoots, leaves, and flower structures. Farmers should look for insects, feeding marks, and the pattern of damage.

**Other fungal diseases:** Some fungal pathogens can cause flower blight, twig dieback, or leaf spots that resemble anthracnose.

**Nutrient deficiencies and environmental stress:** Poor nutrition, drought, excessive moisture, and chemical injury may cause abnormal leaf coloration or tissue damage.

A useful field assessment should include:

1. Identifying which plant parts are affected.
2. Recording when symptoms first appeared.
3. Checking whether damage followed rainfall or insect activity.
4. Examining both healthy and affected trees.
5. Taking clear photographs and recording the location of affected trees.
6. Seeking assistance from an agricultural extension officer or plant disease specialist when the cause remains uncertain.

Laboratory examination may be necessary to confirm the pathogen.

Farmers should avoid selecting a fungicide solely from the appearance of a leaf spot.

## 6. Integrated Management of Cashew Anthracnose

Integrated disease management combines preventive practices, regular monitoring, sanitation, and appropriate interventions when needed.

### 6.1 Regular orchard inspection

Inspect young shoots, leaves, flower clusters, and developing apples regularly.

Increase attention after periods of rainfall or when new growth and flowering coincide with wet weather.

Record the location, severity, and progression of suspicious symptoms.

### 6.2 Orchard sanitation

Remove severely affected shoots or branches when appropriate and dispose of infected material safely.

Clean pruning tools between affected trees to reduce the risk of transferring plant pathogens.

Avoid leaving heavily diseased material in locations where it could contribute to further infection.

### 6.3 Canopy and airflow management

Maintain a well-managed canopy through appropriate pruning.

Improved air movement and sunlight penetration may help reduce prolonged wetness within the canopy.

Avoid excessive pruning, particularly during stressful weather conditions.

### 6.4 Drainage and water management

Maintain suitable orchard drainage and avoid prolonged waterlogging.

Where irrigation is used, consider methods that minimize unnecessary wetting of foliage.

### 6.5 Healthy planting materials

Use healthy planting materials from reliable sources.

Inspect nursery seedlings before planting and avoid introducing plants showing unexplained disease symptoms.

### 6.6 Biological control research

Researchers have investigated biological control organisms against fungi associated with cashew anthracnose.

A 2020 laboratory study in India found that selected *Trichoderma* species inhibited the growth of *Colletotrichum gloeosporioides* under laboratory conditions.

A 2024 study in Côte d'Ivoire also reported reductions in disease severity using experimental bacterial biological-control formulations in greenhouse and orchard trials.

These findings are promising, but their effectiveness depends on the specific formulation, local conditions, and application methods.

They do not mean that any commercially available biological product will control anthracnose in Cambodian orchards.

### 6.7 Responsible fungicide use

Fungicides may form part of an integrated disease management program when the disease has been correctly identified and treatment is justified.

Farmers should use only products authorized for the relevant crop and purpose under Cambodian requirements.

They should follow official label instructions, protective equipment requirements, environmental precautions, and applicable pre-harvest intervals.

Specific chemical products or application rates should not be adopted directly from foreign research without local regulatory and technical verification.

## 7. Practical Monitoring for Cambodian Cashew Growers

A simple monitoring system can help farmers detect suspicious symptoms early and make better management decisions.

**Step 1 — Identify monitoring areas**

Select representative trees from different parts of the orchard, including areas with different drainage or canopy conditions.

**Step 2 — Inspect vulnerable plant parts**

Observe tender leaves, growing shoots, flower clusters, and developing apples.

**Step 3 — Record symptoms**

Note the date, weather conditions, affected plant parts, and approximate extent of damage.

**Step 4 — Compare possible causes**

Check for insect feeding, other disease symptoms, or environmental problems before assuming anthracnose.

**Step 5 — Apply appropriate preventive measures**

Maintain orchard sanitation, suitable drainage, and good canopy management.

**Step 6 — Seek technical confirmation**

If symptoms spread rapidly, affect many trees, or damage flowering and fruit development, contact a qualified agricultural specialist for diagnosis and management advice.

**Step 7 — Review the outcome**

Continue monitoring after any management action to determine whether symptoms are spreading or stabilizing.

These steps can be adapted to different orchard sizes and production systems.

## 8. What Research Tells Us—and What Remains Uncertain

Scientific research confirms that anthracnose is an important disease of cashew in several producing regions.

A 2022 review identified *Colletotrichum* among the major fungal groups associated with cashew diseases and highlighted the need for accurate pathogen identification.

Research in Brazil has demonstrated that multiple *Colletotrichum* species can be associated with cashew anthracnose.

Studies from India and Côte d'Ivoire have investigated biological control approaches, with some encouraging experimental results.

However, findings from other countries should not be interpreted as proof of the prevalence, severity, or treatment effectiveness of anthracnose in Cambodia.

Locally confirmed pathogen identification, disease surveillance, and field evaluations would help establish management recommendations specifically suited to Cambodian cashew production.

## Conclusion

Cashew anthracnose is a fungal disease that can damage leaves, young shoots, flowers, and developing fruits.

Early detection is important, but symptoms alone cannot reliably distinguish anthracnose from insect injuries, other fungal diseases, or environmental stress.

For Cambodian cashew growers, the most practical starting points are regular monitoring, orchard sanitation, suitable pruning, drainage management, and obtaining a reliable diagnosis before selecting treatments.

An integrated approach can help protect orchard health while avoiding unnecessary or inappropriate pesticide use.
