# Cashew Intercropping: Selecting Suitable Crops, Managing Competition, and Improving Farm Income

## 1. Introduction

Cashew is an important agricultural crop in Cambodia, providing income for many farming households. However, cashew trees normally require several years to reach their full production potential. During the early years, the spaces between tree rows may remain underused.

Intercropping is the practice of growing additional crops between the rows of a main crop. In cashew orchards, this approach can help farmers make better use of available land, diversify agricultural production, and generate additional income.

Successful intercropping requires careful planning. Farmers must select suitable crops, manage competition for sunlight, water, and nutrients, and protect the growth and productivity of their cashew trees.

## 2. Benefits and Limitations of Cashew Intercropping

Intercropping can provide several benefits when properly managed.

**Potential benefits include:**

- Additional income from crops harvested before or between cashew harvests.
- Better use of land and sunlight, particularly in young orchards.
- More diverse farm production and reduced dependence on a single crop.
- Reduced weed growth where intercrops provide suitable ground cover.
- Opportunities to improve soil management through crop residues and appropriate legume cultivation.

However, intercropping also involves challenges. Additional crops may compete with cashew trees for moisture, nutrients, and light. They may also increase labor requirements, production costs, or pest and disease management needs.

Intercropping should therefore be treated as a planned farming system rather than simply planting any available crop between cashew trees.

## 3. Selecting Suitable Intercrops

The most suitable intercrop depends on orchard age, tree spacing, canopy cover, soil conditions, rainfall, available labor, and market demand.

### 3.1 Short-duration crops for young orchards

Young cashew orchards generally have more open space and sunlight between tree rows. Depending on local conditions, farmers may consider:

- **Cowpea and other suitable legumes:** Short-duration crops that can provide food or income and, when properly managed, contribute to soil fertility.
- **Yardlong bean:** A vegetable crop that may be suitable where sufficient sunlight, water, and market access are available.
- **Okra:** A short-duration vegetable crop that can be considered in open, well-managed orchard spaces.
- **Pineapple:** A possible intercrop where growing conditions, space, and markets are suitable.

These crops are options for evaluation, not universal recommendations for every orchard.

### 3.2 Crops for partially shaded orchards

As cashew trees mature, their canopies become larger and reduce the sunlight reaching the ground.

Research from India has investigated crops such as turmeric, ginger, and selected tuber crops under cashew trees.

A study in humid tropical Kerala reported that turmeric produced 7.63 tonnes per hectare under mature cashew plantations, compared with 7.01 tonnes per hectare in the open-field treatment. Ginger yield, however, was lower under cashew than in the open field.

These results demonstrate that different crops respond differently to shade. They do not establish expected yields for Cambodian orchards.

Farmers should test shade-tolerant crops on a small area before expanding production.

## 4. Orchard Age, Tree Spacing, and Crop Arrangement

The suitability of intercropping changes as cashew trees grow.

**Young orchards:** Wider spaces between tree rows generally provide better opportunities for short-duration intercrops. Farmers should maintain a clear area around young cashew trees to protect their roots and reduce competition.

**Developing orchards:** As tree canopies expand, farmers should reassess sunlight availability and adjust intercrop selection or planting area.

**Mature orchards:** Dense canopy cover may limit crop choices. Shade-tolerant crops may be considered where sufficient light reaches the ground and soil conditions remain suitable.

Intercrops should not obstruct access for pruning, pest monitoring, harvesting, or orchard maintenance.

Farmers should also avoid deep cultivation close to cashew trunks because it may damage tree roots.

There is no single planting distance suitable for every cashew-intercropping system. Appropriate spacing should be determined by tree age, canopy size, intercrop characteristics, and local agricultural advice.

## 5. Managing Competition for Water, Nutrients, and Sunlight

Cashew trees and intercrops share the same growing environment. Poor management can reduce the performance of one or both crops.

### Water management

During the rainy season, farmers should ensure that soil drainage remains adequate. Cashew trees do not tolerate prolonged waterlogging.

During dry periods, intercrops may compete with cashew trees for limited soil moisture. Farmers should avoid introducing water-demanding intercrops without reliable water availability.

### Nutrient management

Intercrops remove nutrients from the soil when harvested. Farmers should consider the nutrient requirements of both cashew and intercrops.

Organic matter, suitable compost, and fertilizer recommendations based on soil testing can help maintain soil fertility.

Legumes may contribute nitrogen through biological nitrogen fixation under suitable conditions, but they should not be assumed to replace all fertilizer requirements.

### Sunlight management

Vegetables that require strong sunlight may perform poorly under dense cashew canopies.

Farmers should observe shade patterns throughout the day and choose crops that match the available light.

## 6. Pest, Disease, and Soil Management

Intercropping can change the orchard environment and influence pest and disease conditions.

Farmers should regularly inspect both cashew trees and intercrops for insect damage, disease symptoms, and abnormal plant growth.

Crop selection should consider whether an intercrop could host pests or diseases affecting other crops on the farm.

Good practices include maintaining appropriate plant spacing, removing severely diseased plant material safely, keeping harvesting areas accessible, and avoiding unnecessary pesticide applications.

When pesticides are required, farmers must follow the product label, permitted crop uses, safety precautions, and pre-harvest intervals. Chemicals used on intercrops must not contaminate cashew nuts or nearby crops.

Soil management is also important. Farmers should minimize unnecessary soil disturbance and retain appropriate ground cover to reduce erosion.

## 7. Evaluating Farm Costs and Income

Intercropping may create additional income, but higher production does not always mean higher profit.

Farmers should calculate the additional costs of seeds or planting materials, land preparation, fertilizer, irrigation, labor, pest management, harvesting, and transport.

A simple calculation is:

**Net income from the intercrop = Total intercrop sales − Total intercrop production costs**

For example, if a farmer earns USD 600 from an intercrop and spends USD 350 on its production, the estimated net income is USD 250, before accounting for any additional costs not included in the calculation.

This is an illustrative example, not a market-price forecast.

Farmers should also monitor whether intercropping affects cashew tree growth, nut yield, or orchard maintenance costs. A complete comparison should consider the combined financial performance of cashew and intercrops over time.

## 8. Practical Recommendations for Cambodian Farmers

Before adopting intercropping on a large scale, Cambodian cashew growers should follow a practical evaluation process.

1. **Assess the orchard:** Record tree age, spacing, canopy cover, soil conditions, and water availability.
2. **Identify market opportunities:** Select crops with realistic demand, accessible buyers, and manageable transport requirements.
3. **Start with a small trial:** Test one or two suitable intercrops rather than converting the entire orchard immediately.
4. **Protect cashew trees:** Keep sufficient space around trunks and roots, and avoid excessive competition for water and nutrients.
5. **Monitor results:** Record intercrop yields, production costs, labor requirements, and cashew tree performance.
6. **Compare before expanding:** Increase the planting area only when the trial demonstrates acceptable crop performance and economic benefits.

Farmers in different Cambodian provinces may obtain different results because rainfall, soil type, farm management, and market conditions vary.

## Conclusion

Cashew intercropping offers an opportunity to improve land use and diversify farm income, particularly during the early years of orchard development.

However, successful intercropping depends on selecting appropriate crops and managing competition for sunlight, moisture, and nutrients.

For Cambodian farmers, small-scale trials, accurate cost records, and regular observation of cashew tree performance are essential before expanding an intercropping system.

Intercropping should support the long-term health and productivity of the cashew orchard, rather than compromise it.
